\centering
{\rmfamily\itshape\fontsize{56}{60}\selectfont\color{accent} Lakȟótiyapi}\\[4pt]
{\fontsize{20}{24}\selectfont\color{midgray} \emph{the language under test}}
\vspace{16pt}

\begin{block}{Why test this?}
\lak{Lakȟótiyapi} (Lakota) is a Siouan language UNESCO classifies as \textbf{critically endangered} --- fewer than 2,000 first-language speakers, most over 65.
\medskip

Today's LLMs advertise translation across dozens of languages, but training-data coverage is undisclosed. Users reasonably assume a supported language works. For an endangered language with scarce digitized text, that assumption is \textbf{untestable without direct evaluation}.
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Setup}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{7 models.} Proprietary: Gemini 3.1 Pro, Claude Opus 4.6, Claude Sonnet 4.6, GPT-5.2. Open-weight: DeepSeek-V4-Pro, Qwen3.6-Plus, GLM-5.1.
\item \textbf{200 sentence pairs} from the New Lakota Dictionary, conversational register, \textbf{both directions}.
\item \textbf{Two conditions:} baseline vs.\ extended reasoning, where the API permits.
\item \textbf{Metrics:} chrF++ and BLEU; diacritic-normalized chrF++; an LLM \emph{semantic} judge on the English side.
\end{itemize}
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Why two judges?}
chrF++ rewards surface overlap; it cannot distinguish fluent-but-wrong from correct. We judge \emph{meaning} with an LLM, English-to-English (no Lakota knowledge required). To rule out family-level self-judging bias, every pair is judged by \textbf{two models from different families} --- Gemini 3 Flash and the independent Llama-3.3-70B --- which agree substantially: Cohen's $\kappa=0.75$ (0.89 quadratic-weighted).
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Standard Lakota Orthography}
SLO marks phonemic distinctions with diacritics that models often \emph{drop or rearrange}:
\smallskip
\begin{itemize}
\item \lak{ȟ} (caron) vs.\ \lak{h} --- distinct consonants
\item \lak{ą} \lak{į} \lak{ų} (ogonek) --- nasalized vowels
\item \lak{á} (acute) --- stress
\item \lak{ŋ} --- velar nasal
\item \lak{ʼ} --- glottal stop
\end{itemize}
\smallskip
Stripping the marks adds \textbf{+3.3 to +6.5 chrF++} on English\,$\rightarrow$\,Lakota --- isolating orthographic from lexical error: models get the base characters roughly right and the marks inconsistent.
\end{block}

\end{block}

\begin{block}{Reasoning buys calibration, not capability}
For the open-weight models, extended reasoning leaves translation quality and meaning \emph{flat} but raises \textbf{refusals}:
\smallskip
\begin{itemize}
\item Qwen3.6-Plus, E\,$\rightarrow$\,L: \textbf{0\,$\rightarrow$\,36} refusals (of 200) when reasoning is enabled.
\item GLM-5.1, L\,$\rightarrow$\,E: \textbf{1\,$\rightarrow$\,16}.
\end{itemize}
\smallskip
Reasoning surfaces the limitation rather than overcoming it. On a language this far outside the training distribution, the binding constraint is \textbf{knowledge, not inference} --- reasoning's contribution is calibration, not capability.
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Takeaways}
\begin{itemize}
\item No frontier LLM reliably translates Lakota in either direction (as of 2026).
\item \textbf{chrF++ overstates} low-resource capability --- pair it with an LLM semantic judge.
\item Open-weight models do not close the gap; reasoning changes \emph{refusals}, not quality.
\end{itemize}
\end{block}
